Picture Pen

Write a number.
See a picture.

A calm, reward-based writing app for a 4-year-old autistic learner who loves pictures. Built for an Android tablet and stylus.

The brief

Teach writing with what he loves: pictures

Who and where

A 4-year-old autistic boy, starting to write. He loves images. He'll use a Lenovo tablet with a stylus.

The reward

On each success, exactly one picture with its spelling, drawn from 300 pictures suited to his age. No picture bank to choose from.

What to write

Numbers 1–9 first. Capital letters A–Z are added later.

Grows with him

At first: big characters with lots of room for error. As he masters it, the room shrinks, characters get smaller, and more appear on each screen.

For the parent

Stars for every character, so you can see where he is doing well and where he needs practice.

What the research says

Six findings that shaped the design

Screen design

Simple, clear, consistent

A systematic review of autism learning apps groups good practice into simplicity, visual design, feedback and support, engagement, accessibility, and designing with users.

Motivation

His interests are the best rewards

Access to a child's strong interests is an effective reinforcer for autistic children, and clinicians increasingly build on interests rather than suppress them.

Teaching

Lots of help, then fade it

Most-to-least prompting is often the most efficient way to fade help, though no single method always wins. So help must be adjustable.

Tablets

Skills can carry over to paper

Small studies are encouraging: 3 young children with developmental disabilities wrote letters with pencil after LetterSchool lessons; a 10-week iPad program raised visual-motor scores.

Handwriting in autism

Bigger, more variable strokes

Autistic children often write larger, with more variable, faster movements. So the app uses big letters and a forgiving path.

Development

Lines → circle → cross → diagonals

Occupational therapy guides put lines at about 2–3, circle about 3, cross about 4, diagonals and square 4–5. The app's levels follow this order.

Design principles

Eight rules every screen follows

1

One thing per screen

One task, one big button, calm colours, nothing flashing.

2

Same routine, every time

"First write → Then picture" is shown before he starts.

3

Nothing to get wrong

Off the line, the ink just waits. No buzzers, crosses or lost stars.

4

One picture per success

Exactly one, straight away, with its spelling. No gallery to pick from.

5

Always something new

300 everyday pictures, shuffled. None repeats until all 300 have been seen.

6

Grows with him

Big and forgiving at first. Tighter, smaller and more per screen as he masters it.

7

Made for a stylus

Palm guard while the pen is used, hover ring, and the ink follows the pen tip.

8

Safe by design

Full screen, back button ignored, grown-ups area needs a 2-second hold. No ads, no internet, no data leaves the tablet.

Short: about a minute per turn and 3–8 turns per session, so it ends before attention runs out.

Predictable: the steps never change order, and the level only changes between sessions.

Positive: there is no fail state anywhere in the loop, only "not yet".

Mock screen 2 · Trace (Level 1)

Trace: big, forgiving, and impossible to get wrong

1 2 3 4 5 6
  1. Numbered green dotWhere to put the pen, and which stroke comes next.
  2. Arrows + finish ringWhich way to go and where to stop.
  3. "Show me" dotDraws the stroke first, and again if he is stuck for 7 seconds.
  4. Blue inkOnly moves forward along the right path. If the pen wanders, it simply waits.
  5. Name + voice"Number 4": the voice says "four" as it appears.
  6. StarsHow many turns are left today.

Mock screen 3 · Grows with him

As he masters it: tighter path, smaller characters, more per screen

Level 1One number, very big, lots of room. Arrows, dots and demo.

Level 3Two numbers per screen, a little smaller, less room.

Level 6Three smaller numbers, tight path, faint guide only.

Automatic: he moves up a level after 5 turns averaging 4★, and steps back after 3 turns under 2★, so it stays fun. Changes only happen between sessions, never mid-practice. You can also fix the level yourself.

Mock screen 6 · Grown-ups

Stars per character: where he's doing well, where he needs practice

  • Doing well / Needs practice: the strongest and weakest characters at a glance.
  • A card for every character: green 4–5★, yellow 3★, red 1–2★.
  • Average of the last 5 tries, with the number of tries and a trend arrow (↑ getting better).
  • Level and history further down the page.

How a try is scored. Each try gets 1–5★ from how closely the pen stayed on the path (compared with the room at his level), minus a little each time it wandered right off, or "Show me" help was needed.

Mock screen 7 · Grown-ups

Level, history and settings

  • Level ladder 1–7 with what each level means, and how close he is to the next one.
  • Automatic or "I choose": let it grow with him, or fix a level.
  • Recent sessions: date, level, turns and average stars.
  • What to practise: numbers 1–9 (default), letters A–Z, or both. In order, mixed, or more of the weak ones.
  • Help & senses: "Show me" demo, voice, chimes, calm motion, stylus palm guard.

How it's built · tracing engine

How the app knows he traced the letter correctly

  1. Letters are dataEach shape is a list of strokes in a 100 × 100 box, in the order children are taught. Sampled every 1 unit.
  2. Start at the headThe pen must land near the blue or green dot. Anywhere else just makes the dot pulse.
  3. Look ahead, never backThe engine finds the closest point up to 22 units ahead. If it is inside the room allowed at his level (18 at Level 1 down to 6 at Level 7), the ink moves there.
  4. Order and direction for freeProgress only goes forward, so stroke order and direction are always right.
  5. Wandering = pauseToo far away and the ink waits (counted for the stars). Coming back to the dot picks it up again.

Other apps use pixel masks ("colour inside the letter") or per-stroke paths with checkpoints. Per-stroke paths are what make demos, arrows and stroke order possible.

The goal

From tablet to pencil and paper, one small step at a time

Now · built

Trace with a stylus

Holding a pen, not a finger. Guides fade and characters shrink as he masters each level.

Pen pressure is measured with an active pen (like the Lenovo Tab Pen) and shown to you as light, comfortable or pressing hard.

Next · v0.4

Write without a path

Free-write levels: only the writing lines and a start dot. The app checks the shape afterwards.

Paper-size level: characters as big as school lines. Pencil-look ink that darkens with pressure.

Next · v0.4

Paper turns

Printable sheets of his weak characters: dotted to trace, then blank.

When he writes one on paper, you tap "Done on paper" and he gets his one picture, just like on the tablet.

Alongside

Real pencil, real hands

A short, chunky or triangular pencil is easier to grip. Start with "tablet first, then one paper turn".

His OT can check grip and pressure; the stars and pressure panel give them data.

The same routine and the same reward on tablet and paper, so moving to paper feels familiar rather than new.

Getting it on the Lenovo tablet

Install it, then keep him inside it

Install

  1. Get the fileDownload picture-pen.apk on the tablet (download link).
  2. Allow the installOpen it and allow installs from this source. Android may warn that it's not from the Play Store: tap Install anyway.
  3. Open Picture PenIt starts on Numbers 1–9, Level 1.

Set up for him

  1. Pair the stylusThe palm guard turns on by itself once the pen touches the screen.
  2. Turn on App pinningIn Settings, search for "App pinning" and turn it on. Then pin Picture Pen from the recent-apps screen.
  3. Check the Grown-ups areaPress and hold the gear for 2 seconds. Set the turns and voice to suit him.

The app works fully offline and stores everything on the tablet.
